Fibermaxxing: is more fibre always better for your gut?

GutWell Editorial Team

More fibre feeds your gut microbes, and a large increase can also mean more gas. The research is clearer about the first part than the second, and clearest of all that people respond differently.

"Fibermaxxing" - deliberately eating much more fibre than usual - has been going round social media for months. The question underneath it is a fair one. If fibre is good for your gut, is more of it better? Here is what the research actually supports, and where it stops.

What fibre does once you have eaten it

Fibre is the part of plant food your own digestive system mostly cannot break down. It reaches your large intestine more or less intact, and the bacteria living there feed on it. As they do, they produce compounds called short-chain fatty acids. That is the mechanism behind most of fibre's reputation, and it is well established.

"Fibre" on a label is a category, though, not a single ingredient. Some kinds dissolve in water - the sort in oats, beans, barley and some fruit - and gut bacteria get at those more easily than the kinds that do not dissolve. So two foods with the same fibre number on the packet can behave quite differently once you have eaten them.

What happened when researchers actually tried it

In one trial, healthy adults ate a high-fibre diet for seventeen weeks, with eighteen people in each group. Their gut bacteria did adapt: the microbes got measurably better equipped to digest fibre, while the overall mix of species stayed much the same.

Two things about that trial matter more than the headline. The first is that the main thing the researchers set out to measure - a marker of immune activity - did not change. The second is that the high-fibre group did not move together. The researchers found three distinct patterns of response, and which pattern a person followed was related to what their gut bacteria looked like before the study started. Same diet, different outcomes, partly predictable from where each person began.

More fibre can mean more gas

This is the part the trend tends to leave out. In a four-week trial, 48 adults took a daily supplement that added about 44 grams of fibre to their diet - a large amount, and from a supplement rather than from food. They reported more wind than usual. Other symptoms were not consistently worse than in the comparison group, and nobody dropped out because of pain or discomfort. So: a real, measured increase in gas at a high supplemental dose, reported by the participants themselves.

Why gas sometimes becomes discomfort

There is a sensible explanation for that, and it is worth being clear about how much of it is settled. Gut bacteria produce gas as they feed. If gas builds up faster than your gut clears it, the gut stretches, and that stretching is closely tied to the feeling people describe as bloating. In people with irritable bowel syndrome, research points to sensitivity to that stretching - rather than the sheer amount of gas - as what produces symptoms.

Researchers have also tested whether the speed of all this can be slowed on purpose, by giving a quickly-fermented fibre together with a gel-forming one. In thirty healthy volunteers it could: fermentation was delayed rather than prevented. But that trial measured the chemistry, not how anyone felt. Its authors suggest the approach might help people with IBS tolerate fibre better, and they put that forward as something to study next, not as something they had shown.

So the honest version is this: the link between fast fermentation and discomfort is reasonable and partly measured, and whether pacing your fibre intake makes you feel better has not been demonstrated in healthy people.

"Increase it gradually" - what that advice is actually based on

You will read everywhere that you should increase fibre slowly. It is worth knowing how thin the formal evidence for that is.

The most thorough relevant guideline is the British Dietetic Association's, on managing long-term constipation through diet. It was built from four systematic reviews covering 75 trials. It does contain a gradual-increase statement - and it is narrower than the way the advice is usually repeated. It is about fibre supplements rather than food. It applies to people with long-term constipation who already find fibre hard to tolerate. And the guideline itself labels it expert opinion rather than an evidence-based recommendation, which is a distinction its authors draw deliberately: other statements in the same document are labelled evidence-based.

The same guideline excluded studies that simply advised people to eat more fibre, and made no recommendations at all about whole diets, saying the evidence was not there.

So there is no general, evidence-based recommendation that healthy people should increase fibre gradually. Going slower is a reasonable precaution and it follows from the mechanism above. It is not a research finding, and this article is not going to present it as one.

Why the same amount of fibre affects people differently

The seventeen-week trial is the clearest illustration: one diet, three different patterns of response, related to where each person's gut bacteria started. Your starting point matters, and nobody's starting point is the average.

Which is also why a number from an article - this one included - is a place to start rather than an answer. What no general recommendation can tell you is how your own digestion responds to a change you make.

What this cannot tell you

Six papers sit behind this article: two reviews, cited only for how fibre and gut bacteria interact; three trials; and one clinical guideline. The trials are all small - eighteen people per group, 48 people, thirty people - and one of them was run in a single community, with the fibre coming from a supplement rather than food. The guideline's gradual-increase statement is its authors' expert opinion, and applies to fibre supplements in people with long-term constipation. Three of the six papers are not freely available, so they were assessed from their abstracts rather than in full. None of this predicts what will happen for any one person. No paper cited here tested whether drinking more water changes how fibre is tolerated, or whether increasing fibre slowly makes healthy people feel better, so this article claims neither.
